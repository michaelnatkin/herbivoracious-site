Tofu gets a bad reputation mostly because it is often cooked badly: left wet, cooked at too low a heat, and served with a sauce that never gets near the inside. Fix those three things and it becomes one of the most useful ingredients in a vegetarian kitchen.

## Pick the right tofu

- **Firm or extra-firm** is the one for pan-searing, baking, grilling and stir-frying. It holds its shape.
- **Soft and silken** tofu is for soups, sauces, dressings, smoothies and desserts. It will fall apart in a hot pan.
- **Pressed or baked tofu**, sold at some stores and tofu shops, has already had much of its water removed, so it browns very quickly. I wrote about <a href="/2012/01/pan-seared-pressed-tofu-with-apples-and-champagne-vinaigrette-recipe/">pan-seared pressed tofu with apples and champagne vinaigrette</a> after bringing some home from a local tofu maker.

## Press it

Water is the enemy of crisp tofu. Drain the block, wrap it in a clean kitchen towel or a few layers of paper towel, set it on a plate, and put something heavy on top: a cast-iron pan, or a few cans. Give it 20 to 30 minutes, then pat it dry again. You can skip a press if you like. Just do not skip the draining.

## Three ways to cook it

**Pan-sear.** Cut the pressed tofu into slabs or cubes. Heat a wide skillet (cast iron or nonstick) over medium-high heat with enough oil to coat the bottom. Add the tofu in a single layer, leave it alone for 3 to 4 minutes until deeply golden, then turn and brown the other sides. Crowding the pan or flipping early is what makes it stick and steam.

**Dust it for extra crunch.** Toss the cubes in a spoonful or two of cornstarch with a pinch of salt before they go in the pan. It creates a thin shell that fries up crisp, and it also helps sauce cling.

**Bake.** Toss cubes in a little oil and salt, spread them on a sheet pan and bake at 425°F (220°C), turning once, for about 25 to 30 minutes until golden at the edges. It is the easiest hands-off method.

## Getting flavor into it

Plain tofu is a blank canvas, and a marinade only really flavors the surface. These approaches work better:

1. **Sear first, sauce at the end.** Brown the tofu, then toss it in a hot sauce for the last minute so it glazes. This is the idea behind <a href="/2011/11/caramel-cooked-tofu-recipe/">caramel-cooked tofu</a>.
2. **Use a bold sauce.** Chili, ginger, garlic, black bean, miso. See <a href="/2013/08/mission-chinese-style-tofu-with-radish-and-shiso-recipe/">Mission Chinese-style tofu with radish and shiso</a>, <a href="/2009/03/asparagus-and-tofu-with-guilin-chili-sauce/">asparagus and tofu with Guilin chili sauce</a>, or <a href="/2011/09/sichuan-dry-fried-green-beans-and-tofu-recipe/">Sichuan dry-fried green beans and tofu</a>.
3. **Glaze and broil.** <a href="/2011/06/tofu-dengaku-recipe/">Tofu dengaku</a> is tofu topped with a sweet miso glaze.
4. **Put it in something with texture.** <a href="/2008/04/recipe-vietname/">Banh mi chay</a> and <a href="/2010/05/grilled-tofu-and-pepper-vegetarian-tacos-recipe/">grilled tofu tacos</a> pair it with crunchy, fresh and acidic things.

<div class="faq"><h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>Do I have to press tofu?</h3>
<p>For crisp, golden tofu, yes, or at least drain it and pat it very dry. For soups, stews and braises you can skip it.</p>

<h3>Why does my tofu fall apart or stick to the pan?</h3>
<p>Usually the tofu is too soft, too wet, or moved too soon. Use firm or extra-firm tofu, pat it dry, heat the pan well, and let each side brown until it releases on its own.</p>

<h3>Can you eat tofu without cooking it?</h3>
<p>Yes. Packaged tofu is already cooked during manufacturing. Cooking improves its texture and flavor, but cold cubes in a salad or dressing are fine.</p>

<h3>How long does leftover tofu keep?</h3>
<p>Store opened tofu in a container covered with fresh water in the refrigerator, change the water daily, and use it within about five days. Cooked tofu keeps for a few days in a sealed container.</p>
