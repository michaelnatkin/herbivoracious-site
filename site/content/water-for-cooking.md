Water is the most-used ingredient in my kitchen, and for years it was the one I thought about least. It goes into the beans, the stock, the bread dough, the pickle brine and the morning tea. Then I started filtering it, and I haven't gone back.

## Where water quality matters most

**Beans and lentils.** Dried beans are mostly water and time. Very hard water, which is high in calcium and magnesium, can keep them from softening, and chlorinated tap water can come through in a simple pot of beans.

**Bread and ferments.** Chlorine and chloramine are there to kill microbes, and yeast and fermenting bacteria are microbes. Plenty of bakers and fermenters use filtered water for that reason.

**Broth, soup and tea.** When a dish is mostly water, you taste the water. A clean-tasting base is a better starting point for a delicate broth or a pot of tea.

## What I use

I use a {{% product id="cloud-ro-water-filter" target="_blank" %}}Cloud reverse osmosis system{{% /product %}} at home. The water tastes good to me, and I feel better knowing that a lot of contaminants are filtered out, including microplastics.

Reverse osmosis pushes water through a very fine membrane that blocks most dissolved solids and tiny particles. That is why it is widely used where people want the most thorough filtration, as opposed to a pitcher filter that mainly improves taste.

## Things to know before you buy

- **It uses water.** Cloud says the system sends about one gallon to the drain for each gallon it dispenses. That is common for reverse osmosis, and worth weighing.
- **It removes minerals.** Reverse osmosis strips minerals along with contaminants. Cloud adds some back in a final stage, according to their FAQ.
- **Installation.** Cloud says it takes 30 to 60 minutes and offers a video guide.
- **You may not need one.** If chlorine taste is your only complaint, a carbon filter pitcher or faucet filter is much cheaper.
